# 0003: Bodies in liquid, and the push-out from inside a body

Date: 2026-10-12. Status: proposed (Milestone 3 step 4 and the sand push-out the owner chose, 2026-10-12: "a grain inside a body is pushed toward the nearest edge of the body's rest shape, using the body's position and rotation from shape matching"). Written before the code. Items 2 (level, compression), 4 (reference pressure, wet floor) and 6 (drag) were changed or added by the CPU tests, as each says; the measurements are in "Decided by the tests" below.

## Context
ADR 0002 left two things open:
- **Body–liquid coupling (step 4).** Water passes into and fills bodies: body particles are left out of the density projection grid, of water's PBF density, and of the contact rule for body–liquid pairs.
- **Grains inside a body.** A body's lattice spacing is the contact distance, so a grain pushed into a lattice square sits at about 0.7 spacing from four body particles, whose push-outs cancel. An 8 × 6 box dropped 72 px onto sand ends with 7 grains inside on the CPU reference; dropped 100 px on the GPU, 16 (ADR 0002 Known limits). Liquid has the same problem.

What the grid does today, which constrains step 4:
- The density projection grid (`hydro.glslinc`) pushes only liquid particles, by −∇p dt². Each water particle is held up by the grid's gradient, so water doesn't press on what it touches. A body resting on water only gets back the part of its own weight that it squeezes into the water, whatever its density: contacts alone give no buoyancy.
- Cells that are mostly sand are bed: a wall that holds the water's weight (dp/dn = ρg n), with p continued hydrostatically into the pores. A dry bed carries that pressure too (no liquid feels it there).
- Cells are 16 px. A carrot box of 8 × 6 particles is 16 × 12 px, under one cell.

## Decision
**The grid sees bodies (step 4)**
1. **Two grid channels for body particles.** `hydro_splat` adds each body particle to a body-volume sum and, at min(ρ_b, 1), to a body-weight sum; `hydro_phi` becomes a `vec4` per cell (liquid, powder, body volume, body weight) and `hydro_acc` four integers per cell. `hydro_powder_fill` (the drawing) still reads only powder. `hydro_solve` keeps one `vec2` per cell in shared memory, (level, bed fill), and reads a cell's own compression and room and the cell above's fullness from `hydro_phi` when it sets up the cell's row, so it still needs 12 B per cell (16 KB is the Vulkan minimum per workgroup).
2. **Body volume fills cells; body weight sets the level.** A body particle sits on the rest-spacing lattice, so it fills a cell as much as a water particle at rest. In the solve:
   - the **bed test** counts its volume: a cell is a wall that holds the water's weight when powder × `HYDRO_POWDER_VOL` + body ≥ `HYDRO_SOLID`, so water around a big body has hydrostatic pressure and isn't sucked into it;
   - the **bubble pull** counts its volume, in the cell above (`covered`) and in the cell's own room (liquid + powder × `HYDRO_POWDER_FULL` + body), so a gap next to or under a body is closed and the body itself isn't a bubble to fill;
   - **compression** counts its volume (liquid + body over 1), so a film of water under a body is pushed out. Without it a carrot sunk onto the floor rested tilted 0.25 rad on a one-particle film of water and slid along the floor at ~2 px/s;
   - the **air test and the free surface** use the cell's level, `hydro_level`: liquid + body weight × min(liquid / `HYDRO_AIR`, 1). A floating body displaces its own weight of water, so a cell it floats in reads as level as the water beside it. With the body left out of the level, a cork's cell (liquid 0.36, body 0.44) read lower than its neighbours (0.6); p fell under the cork, and wherever it moved, its new cell's p fell too, so the pressure on its trailing side pushed it on: it drifted at 2 px/s and never stopped. The weight counts only where the cell holds liquid, so a dry body's cells stay air and don't read as a column of water.
3. **Water's PBF density counts body particles at share 1** (they fill a water particle's room), as grains count at `LIQUID_POWDER_SHARE`. They add to the constraint's gradient but don't move with it.

**Water presses on bodies (buoyancy)**
4. **Pressure on a body's wet surface.** Once per substep, before the solver iterations (with the grid's push on liquids, `solve_pressure`), each body particle with liquid neighbours is pushed by the grid pressure at its surface:
   Δx = −(p(x + ½ spacing · ĝ) − p_ref) · (g / G) · dt² / (ρ_b · spacing),
   where g = Σ over liquid neighbours of the spiky kernel gradient (it points toward the water, and is longer at a corner, which has water on two sides), ĝ its direction, G the length of g for a flat face of water at rest spacing (`body_face_grad`, computed from the kernel as `rest_density` is), and ρ_b the body's material density (water's density is 1, as everywhere in the grid until per-material liquid density, Milestone 3b). Summed over a body this is ∮ p n dA over its wet surface: a submerged body is pushed up by the weight of the water it displaces (Archimedes), a dry body by nothing, and a floating body only below the waterline, so it rights itself. On the CPU an 8 × 6 cork's pushes summed to 1.26 px per substep against its weight's 1.33. The sample point is the body's outline, half a spacing out from the particle, so the body's height counts once; at the water particles' positions instead, a column of N particles gets (N + 1)/N of its buoyancy. Below the last row of cell centres the sample is continued hydrostatically (`body_p_sample`).
   - **The reference pressure p_ref.** A uniform pressure over a closed surface adds nothing, but deep down it is large: at the floor of a 30k pool (~190 px) the push on each wet particle would be ~2 px per substep, past the half-spacing cap on a correction, so the top and bottom pushes would both be cut and cancel, and a jelly would be squeezed. So a body closed in by liquid, grains, other bodies, walls and the floor is pushed relative to its lowest wet-surface pressure: about ρg × its height. A body with a boundary member in air (nothing but its own body within 1.5 spacings, away from the walls; a boundary member's `e` is half a spacing long) is floating, and its p_ref is 0. Referred to its lowest wet pressure instead, a floating cork that bobbed up until only its bottom row was wet lost all its lift at the top of each bob and kept bobbing at ±8 px/s; smoothing p_ref over 0.1 s made it worse. A dry body's p_ref is +∞ (nothing presses on it).
   - p_ref is the previous substep's: `solve_pressure` takes the minimum over the body's members (wet: their p; a boundary member in air: 0; others: +∞) with an integer `atomicMin` on the float's bits (p ≥ 0, so they order as integers) into the record's last word, and the first body pass of the next substep moves it into `p_ref`. The record's `vec4 rest` becomes rest centroid (`vec2`), `p_ref` (`float`) and that minimum (`uint`), still 48 B.
   - **The floor under a wet body is wet.** A body particle within a spacing of the floor, of a body that was wet in the last substep (`info.w` flag), counts the floor below it as a face of liquid at the grid's pressure there: water seeps under a body. Without it a cork let go on the floor of a pool, wet only on its top and sides, got no net push and stayed there.
5. **Body–liquid contacts follow the body rule with mass weights.** Pairs closer than one spacing are pushed apart, the share by density as between bodies (the light side moves more), with no friction (water has none) and no `STACK_K`. A liquid particle's contact push stays averaged over its contacts. Grains keep `POWDER_LIQUID_SHARE` and `POWDER_LIQUID_REACH`.
6. **Bodies don't get wet, but water drags them.** `WET_SLIP` (friction lost among liquid neighbours) stays a powder rule: a body's friction against grains and other bodies doesn't change under water. A body particle's velocity relaxes toward its liquid neighbours' kernel-weighted velocity at `BODY_LIQUID_DRAG` 20 per second, scaled by how much liquid is around it (`body_liquid_drag`, as `powder_liquid_drag` for grains). Only wet particles feel it, so a body's drag grows with its wet surface while its mass grows with its area. Without it a floating cork heaved at its natural period (~0.8 s, √(g / draft)) and never settled, and a cork rising from the floor capsized at the surface. At 40 per second a rising cork rolled over under water. The water feels no reaction (as for grains).

**Push-out from inside a body (the owner's option 1)**
7. **Each member stores the way out of its body's rest shape**: `e`, the vector from its rest offset to the nearest point of the rest shape's outline, in the body's rest frame. The outline runs half a spacing outside the boundary members (where a lattice cell ends). It is worked out once, on the CPU, when the body is made (`ParticleStep.rest_outline`): boundary members are those with fewer than 8 members within 1.5 spacings; each one's outward normal is the sum of the directions from its neighbours to it; every member takes the nearest outline point among those its neighbours took (a multi-source walk over the member graph from the boundary inward, linear in the body's size). On the GPU, `rest_offset` grows from `vec2` to `vec4` (rest offset, `e`), 16 B per slot, and `body_spawn` carries `e` with each particle.
8. **The push.** In `contact_delta`, a particle that isn't a body member (a grain or a liquid particle) finds its nearest body particle j within one spacing. With R the body's rotation from shape matching (`frame.zw`), E = R e_j, N = E / |E|, its depth inside the outline is d = |E| − N · (x − x_j). If d > 0, it is pushed by d · N, scaled by `CONTACT_RELAX` like the other contacts and not averaged for liquids. Only that particle moves: the body feels the grain through the ordinary contacts. At rest a grain on a body sits a full spacing from its nearest member, so d < 0 (−0.5 spacing on a flat face, about −0.2 beside a corner, whose outward normal is diagonal) and the push-out never touches a resting contact.
9. **Liquid gets the same push-out**, not only grains: a water particle inside a lattice square is stuck for the same reason, and water staying out of bodies is the step 4 goal.
10. **An erased body loses its push-out.** The eraser changes the outline, and the GPU doesn't know which members went. `brush_erase` marks a body whose member it erases (`info.w` bit 0, "outline stale", set with `atomicOr`), and the push-out skips stale bodies; the ordinary contacts still apply. The CPU reference does the same in `erase_particles`. Cutting (SPEC 2.2) would work the outline out again.
11. **Body–body pairs keep the ordinary contact.** Two bodies pushed into each other could use the same outlines (as FleX uses each rigid particle's signed distance for rigid–rigid contacts), but it needs both bodies' frames per pair; left until it shows up.

## Considered and rejected
- **Bodies as liquid volume in the grid** (counted in the liquid channel). The grid can't tell a body from water: a dry body on the floor reads as a water column with hydrostatic pressure, and a floating body as a mound of water up to its top, whose extra pressure under it grows as it rises (worked through on paper, not run).
- **The grid's push on every body particle (−∇p dt² / ρ_b)**, with body cells as bed. Bed cells carry a hydrostatic pressure even when dry (it bootstraps from the floor up), so a dry body on the floor or on dry sand would be lifted. Fixing that in the bed cells changes the tuned sand-and-water scenes, and `hydro_solve` precomputes each cell's update from φ alone, so a rule that depends on p can't go into it.
- **Buoyancy from contacts and mass ratios alone** (FleX 7.1). It relies on depth pressure being carried by PBF compression; here the grid carries it, and water at rest is at rest density, so there is nothing to push the body up.
- **A per-body buoyancy force in the body pass** (count submerged members, push all of them up). Needs a "submerged" test per member, which the grid can't give for small bodies, and loses the righting torque.
- **For the push-out: the direction from the grain to the body centroid, or from the members around it.** The centroid points out the wrong side for a grain near a long box's end; the members around a grain inside a lattice square are what cancel today.
- **The push-out shared with the body (mass-weighted).** The grain would need the count of members it sits among to split the push, which it doesn't know; the ordinary contacts already carry the impact to the body.

## Known limits
- **Floaters ride ~2 px high.** The grid puts p = 0 about 3 px above the top water particles (where a cell's liquid crosses `HYDRO_SURFACE`), and a floating body's pressure is absolute, so it floats level with the grid's surface: an 8 × 6 cork at density 0.5 shows 0.31 of its height under the water's top particles, not 0.5 (CPU).
- **A box only just stable upright rocks.** A box of density ρ floats upright if width / height > √(6ρ(1 − ρ)) (1.22 at 0.5); the 8 × 6 cork is 1.33 and rocks slowly, ±0.1–0.4 rad in a 48 px pool whose slosh keeps moving it. A cork rising from the floor came up fast and turned over once (it floats face down as well).
- Bodies drag water nowhere: the drag acts on the body only, so a moving body doesn't pull the water around it along.
- Body–body overlaps on fast impacts aren't pushed out (item 11).
