# 0003: Bodies in liquid, and the push-out from inside a body

Date: 2026-10-12. Status: accepted, implemented in the CPU reference and on the GPU (Milestone 3 step 4 and the sand push-out the owner chose, 2026-10-12: "a grain inside a body is pushed toward the nearest edge of the body's rest shape, using the body's position and rotation from shape matching"). Written before the code. Items 2 (level, compression), 4 (reference pressure, wet floor) and 6 (drag) were changed or added by the CPU tests, and item 4 (wet face, surface drop) by the GPU probe, as each says; item 12 (a soft body's pushes made rigid) was added after the owner chose it, 2026-10-12, to fix the first Known limit; items 11 and 13 were added 2026-10-13 after the owner reported bodies passing into each other, item 14 (swept contacts between bodies) the same day as the next step agreed with the owner, and item 15 (a body's whole face wet under water, wet side walls) the same day after the owner reported corks staying in a sand bed under water, and item 16 (the grid's cells around a body pinned under sand) the same day after the owner saw such corks shake and the sea jump with them. The measurements are in "Decided by the tests" below.

## Context
ADR 0002 left two things open:
- **Body–liquid coupling (step 4).** Water passes into and fills bodies: body particles are left out of the density projection grid, of water's PBF density, and of the contact rule for body–liquid pairs.
- **Grains inside a body.** A body's lattice spacing is the contact distance, so a grain pushed into a lattice square sits at about 0.7 spacing from four body particles, whose push-outs cancel. An 8 × 6 box dropped 72 px onto sand ends with 7 grains inside on the CPU reference; dropped 100 px on the GPU, 16 (ADR 0002 Known limits). Liquid has the same problem.

What the grid does today, which constrains step 4:
- The density projection grid (`hydro.glslinc`) pushes only liquid particles, by −∇p dt². Each water particle is held up by the grid's gradient, so water doesn't press on what it touches. A body resting on water only gets back the part of its own weight that it squeezes into the water, whatever its density: contacts alone give no buoyancy.
- Cells that are mostly sand are bed: a wall that holds the water's weight (dp/dn = ρg n), with p continued hydrostatically into the pores. A dry bed carries that pressure too (no liquid feels it there).
- Cells are 16 px. A carrot box of 8 × 6 particles is 16 × 12 px, under one cell.

## Decision
**The grid sees bodies (step 4)**
1. **Two grid channels for body particles.** `hydro_splat` adds each body particle to a body-volume sum and, at min(ρ_b, 1), to a body-weight sum; `hydro_phi` becomes a `vec4` per cell (liquid, powder, body volume, body weight) and `hydro_acc` four integers per cell. `hydro_powder_fill` (the drawing) still reads only powder. `hydro_solve` keeps one `vec2` per cell in shared memory, (level, bed fill), and reads a cell's own compression and room and the cell above's fullness from `hydro_phi` when it sets up the cell's row, so it still needs 12 B per cell (16 KB is the Vulkan minimum per workgroup).
2. **Body volume fills cells; body weight sets the level.** A body particle sits on the rest-spacing lattice, so it fills a cell as much as a water particle at rest. In the solve:
   - the **bed test** counts its volume: a cell is a wall that holds the water's weight when powder × `HYDRO_POWDER_VOL` + body ≥ `HYDRO_SOLID`, so water around a big body has hydrostatic pressure and isn't sucked into it;
   - the **bubble pull** counts its volume, in the cell above (`covered`) and in the cell's own room (liquid + powder × `HYDRO_POWDER_FULL` + body), so a gap next to or under a body is closed and the body itself isn't a bubble to fill;
   - **compression** counts its volume (liquid + body over 1), so a film of water under a body is pushed out. Without it a carrot sunk onto the floor rested tilted 0.25 rad on a one-particle film of water and slid along the floor at ~2 px/s;
   - the **air test and the free surface** use the cell's level, `hydro_level`: liquid + body weight × min(liquid / `HYDRO_AIR`, 1). A floating body displaces its own weight of water, so a cell it floats in reads as level as the water beside it. With the body left out of the level, a cork's cell (liquid 0.36, body 0.44) read lower than its neighbours (0.6); p fell under the cork, and wherever it moved, its new cell's p fell too, so the pressure on its trailing side pushed it on: it drifted at 2 px/s and never stopped. The weight counts only where the cell holds liquid, so a dry body's cells stay air and don't read as a column of water.
3. **Water's PBF density counts body particles at share 1** (they fill a water particle's room), as grains count at `LIQUID_POWDER_SHARE`. They add to the constraint's gradient but don't move with it.

**Water presses on bodies (buoyancy)**
4. **Pressure on a body's wet surface.** Once per substep, before the solver iterations (with the grid's push on liquids, `solve_pressure`), each body particle with liquid neighbours is pushed by the grid pressure at its surface:
   Δx = −(p(x + ½ spacing · ĝ) − p_ref) · g · dt² / (ρ_b · spacing),
   where g is the particle's **wet face**: the sum over its liquid neighbours of the unit vector to each, weighted 1 out to 1.5 spacings and fading to 0 at H, over 1 + √2 (`body_wet_face`). It reads 1 for a flat face of liquid on the rest lattice (one neighbour straight on, two diagonal) and √2 along the diagonal at a corner wet on two sides, wherever the water sits within 1.5 spacings. ĝ is its direction, and ρ_b the body's material density (water's density is 1, as everywhere in the grid until per-material liquid density, Milestone 3b). Pressure under p_ref pushes nothing (the push never pulls a body toward the water). Summed over a body this is ∮ p n dA over its wet surface: a submerged body is pushed up by the weight of the water it displaces (Archimedes), a dry body by nothing, and a floating body only below the waterline, so it rights itself. The sample point is the body's outline, half a spacing out from the particle, so the body's height counts once; at the water particles' positions instead, a column of N particles gets (N + 1)/N of its buoyancy. Below the last row of cell centres the sample is continued hydrostatically (`body_p_sample`).
   - *Changed by the GPU probe:* the first version summed spiky kernel gradients over their value for a flat lattice face (`body_face_grad`). Water by a body sits ~2–2.5 px off it and not on a lattice, and the spiky gradient falls steeply over that range, so it read 0.55–0.64 of a flat face, and an 80 × 40 cork floated at 0.71 of its height instead of ~0.5 (`body_probe waterbig`).
   - **The reference pressure p_ref.** A uniform pressure over a closed surface adds nothing, but deep down it is large: at the floor of a 30k pool (~190 px) the push on each wet particle would be ~2 px per substep, past the half-spacing cap on a correction, so the top and bottom pushes would both be cut and cancel, and a jelly would be squeezed. So a body closed in by liquid, grains, other bodies, walls and the floor is pushed relative to its lowest wet-surface pressure: about ρg × its height. A body with a boundary member in air (nothing but its own body within 1.5 spacings, away from the walls; a boundary member's `e` is half a spacing long) is floating, and its p_ref is 0. Referred to its lowest wet pressure instead, a floating cork that bobbed up until only its bottom row was wet lost all its lift at the top of each bob and kept bobbing at ±8 px/s; smoothing p_ref over 0.1 s made it worse. A dry body's p_ref is +∞ (nothing presses on it).
   - **p_ref is at least g × `BODY_SURFACE_DROP` (2.5 px)**, the height of the grid's free surface (where p reaches 0, as a cell's liquid crosses `HYDRO_SURFACE`) over the surface of the top water particles. With p_ref 0, a floating body's pressure is absolute and it floats level with the grid's surface: an 8 × 6 cork showed 0.16–0.38 of its height under the water's top particles, not 0.5. A max, not a switch at p_min = 0: switched, a 16 × 10 cork whose top went awash (no member in air, p_min ~0) lost the drop, popped up, got it back and sank again, heaving ±4 px for 15 s (`body_probe waterbox`). Deep bodies (p_min ≫ g × 2.5) don't feel it.
   - **A bare member reads the grid, not 0** (*changed 2026-10-13*, after the owner saw a 16 × 10 cork dropped into a full screen of poured water lying on the floor). A boundary member with nothing within 1.5 spacings stands for the body's face out of the water, but in poured water it is more often beside one of the small voids the pour leaves. It now gives the grid's pressure at its outline point (its `e`, turned by the body's rotation) to the minimum instead of 0: past the free surface that is 0, as before, and in a void it is the water's pressure there. Read as 0, a void beside any member set p_ref to 0 for the next substep, and deep down the absolute pushes, ~7 px a substep at 270 px against the half-spacing cap, were cut to the cap on every wet face and cancelled. The cork had no buoyancy and sank, or never left the floor: `body_probe pourfloor` (a cork on the floor, then 4 × "+10k water" 30 frames apart) and `pourdrop` (a turned cork dropped while they pour) ended on the floor under ~240 px of water at draft 12, p_ref at the floor value 1000 in most samples against ~90 000 for the pressure at the cork's top. The same false reading showed in shallow water, where it did no harm: a cork rising from the floor of a 36 px CPU pool had p_ref 1000 from frame 30 while still ~25 px under (`test_submerged_body_keeps_its_reference_pressure`).
   - p_ref is the previous substep's: `solve_pressure` takes the minimum over the body's members (wet: their p; a boundary member in air: 0; others: +∞) with an integer `atomicMin` on the float's bits (p ≥ 0, so they order as integers) into the record's last word, and the first body pass of the next substep moves it into `p_ref`. The record's `vec4 rest` becomes rest centroid (`vec2`), `p_ref` (`float`) and that minimum (`uint`), still 48 B.
   - **The floor under a wet body is wet.** A body particle within 1.5 spacings of the floor (its bottom row rests half a spacing up), of a body that was wet in the last substep (`info.w` flag), counts the floor below it as a face of liquid at the grid's pressure there: water seeps under a body. Without it a cork let go on the floor of a pool, wet only on its top and sides, got no net push and stayed there.
5. **Body–liquid contacts follow the body rule with mass weights.** Pairs closer than one spacing are pushed apart, the share by density as between bodies (the light side moves more), with no friction and no `STACK_K`. The pair runs the ordinary friction with the smaller of the two frictions, which is 0 because water's `friction` is 0; a liquid with friction (Milestone 3b) needs this made a rule. A liquid particle's contact push stays averaged over its contacts. Grains keep `POWDER_LIQUID_SHARE` and `POWDER_LIQUID_REACH`.
6. **Bodies don't get wet, but water drags them.** `WET_SLIP` (friction lost among liquid neighbours) stays a powder rule: a body's friction against grains and other bodies doesn't change under water. A body particle's velocity relaxes toward its liquid neighbours' kernel-weighted velocity at `BODY_LIQUID_DRAG` 20 per second, scaled by how much liquid is around it (`body_liquid_drag`, as `powder_liquid_drag` for grains). Only wet particles feel it, so a body's drag grows with its wet surface while its mass grows with its area. Without it a floating cork heaved at its natural period (~0.8 s, √(g / draft)) and never settled, and a cork rising from the floor capsized at the surface. At 40 per second a rising cork rolled over under water. The water feels no reaction (as for grains).
12. **A soft body's pressure pushes move it as a rigid body.** Between the pushes and their apply, each body whose stiffness is under 1 has its members' pushes (after the half-spacing cap) replaced by their rigid part: the mean push plus a turn about the members' centroid, θ = Σ r × δ / Σ |r|², linearised, each clamped again to half a spacing (`body_project.glsl`, one 128-thread workgroup per body like `body_match`, in its own `bodies` pass between `solve_pressure` and its `solve_apply`; `ParticleStep._project_body_pushes`). The pushes on a wet surface are relative to the body's top (p_ref), so on a 20 px tall jelly the bottom and lower sides are pushed at up to ~9 g and the top hardly at all: their sum is the buoyancy and their torque the righting moment, but the rest squeezed the jelly from the sides, and water got in where it buckled. Members share a material, so their masses are equal. Rigid bodies were left alone, since matching at stiffness 1 takes out the rest within the iteration; item 17 projects them too, and moves the cap after the projection. The pass reads and writes `scratch`, which `solve_pressure` already fills, so `common.glslinc` doesn't change (stamp stays v17).

**Push-out from inside a body (the owner's option 1)**
7. **Each member stores the way out of its body's rest shape**: `e`, the vector from its rest offset to the nearest point of the rest shape's outline, in the body's rest frame. The outline runs half a spacing outside the boundary members (where a lattice cell ends). It is worked out once, on the CPU, when the body is made (`ParticleStep.rest_outline`): boundary members are those with fewer than 8 members within 1.5 spacings; each one's outward normal is the sum of the directions from its neighbours to it; every member takes the nearest outline point among those its neighbours took (a multi-source walk over the member graph from the boundary inward, linear in the body's size). On the GPU, `rest_offset` grows from `vec2` to `vec4` (rest offset, `e`), 16 B per slot, and `body_spawn` carries `e` with each particle.
8. **The push.** In `contact_delta`, a particle that isn't a body member (a grain or a liquid particle) finds its nearest body particle j within one spacing. With R the body's rotation from shape matching (`frame.zw`), E = R e_j, N = E / |E|, its depth inside the outline is d = |E| − N · (x − x_j). If d > 0, it is pushed by d · N, scaled by `CONTACT_RELAX` like the other contacts and not averaged for liquids. Only that particle moves: the body feels the grain through the ordinary contacts. At rest a grain on a body sits a full spacing from its nearest member, so d < 0 (−0.5 spacing on a flat face, about −0.2 beside a corner, whose outward normal is diagonal) and the push-out never touches a resting contact.
9. **Liquid gets the same push-out**, not only grains: a water particle inside a lattice square is stuck for the same reason, and water staying out of bodies is the step 4 goal.
10. **An erased body loses its push-out.** The eraser changes the outline, and the GPU doesn't know which members went. `brush_erase` marks a body whose member it erases (`info.w` bit 0, "outline stale", set with `atomicOr`), and the push-out skips stale bodies; the ordinary contacts still apply. Since ADR 0004 this lasts only until the split that follows the erase: the split works each piece's outline out again and clears the flag (a few frames later on the GPU, at once in the CPU reference's `erase_particles`), also for a body left in one piece.
11. **Body–body pairs get the push-out too** (*changed 2026-10-13*, when the owner reported that solids don't collide properly). A body member takes item 8's push-out from its nearest particle of another body, scaled by its share of their contact (`stack_weight`, by density and centroid height as in the ordinary contact). Each side reads only the other body's frame, and each takes its share, so the pair separates by the depth. A member that has passed the nearest member's edge row (it lies on the inner side of it along the outline's normal) also loses that one pair's ordinary push, which would drive it on inward. Before this, the ordinary contacts were all a body pair had, and they balance among two interleaved lattices: a 16 × 10 carrot dropped 150 px onto another (~240 px/s at the impact, over a spacing per substep) ended with 240 of its 320 members inside the other, for good (`body_probe boxdrop`; the same on the CPU, `test_box_dropped_on_box_stays_out`). Without the edge-row rule, at 4 substeps the same drop locked with the rows 0.27 px apart: the pair pushed 1.73 px in against the outline's 1.27 out. The rule drops only that pair, and only past the edge row: dropping every body pair whenever the push-out acted tilted `stack3` to 0.044 rad, from 0.011, because an offset stack's corner members sit just inside the outline under load.
13. **A push between bodies may stop a body but not send it away** (*changed 2026-10-13*, pre-stabilization as in Macklin et al. 2014, "Unified particle physics for real-time applications"). Each body member adds up, over the iterations of a substep, the part of its correction that comes from other bodies (their contacts and item 11's push-out; `solve_delta`, in the slot buffer `vel`, which is free from `hash_scatter` to `velocity_commit`), and after each shape matching every member holds its body's mean (`body_match`). At the velocity update, along that push's direction n, the body may be slowed to 0 by it but keeps none of it as speed away: v·n becomes v·n − |push|/dt if that is still ≥ 0, else min(v·n, 0). From 330 px (main.tscn's Drop buttons, ~390 px/s, 1.6 spacings a substep at 2 substeps), a 16 × 10 carrot dips ~5 px into the one below for a frame; undoing that came back as speed, it bounced up at 78 px/s, tilted and slid off (`body_probe boxhigh`; CPU `test_fast_box_drop_stacks_off_centre`, 73 px/s). Tried first: a per-member cap relative to the other body (no change: only the bottom row touches, and shape matching hands the whole body its push); taking the whole push out of the speed (the box sank 10 px in: the push is what stops it); the push-out alone without the contacts (a 57 px/s rebound was left). Per body, not per member, for the same reason as the cap.
14. **A body that went into another this substep is rewound along its path to where they first touched** (*added 2026-10-13*, swept contacts as in SunaEngine, `docs/papers.md` "Soft & rigid bodies"). Once per substep, after the hash and before `solve_pressure` (`body_sweep.glsl`, per particle), each body member looks for the other body's member nearest it at the start of the substep (`s_pos`), whose way out of the rest shape (turned by its body's rotation) gives the face it came through: normal N, outline |e| beyond that member (item 8). If the member started at most `SWEEP_DEPTH` (0.25 spacing) inside that face, ends inside the other body's outline (item 8's test at the predicted positions, so a member passing beside a corner doesn't count), and went at least `SWEEP_DEPTH` further in, it went on for the share (d₁ − max(d₀, 0)) / (d₁ − d₀) of the substep after they touched, with d₀, d₁ its depths past the face at the start and end. `body_project` (per body, before the pressure pushes are applied) takes the largest share among a body's members, the first to touch, and moves every member back by that share of its own move this substep (`s_pred − s_pos`), unclamped; it is also the start of the member's push from other bodies (`vel`, item 13). Both bodies rewind the same share of their own paths, so together they undo the overlap: a body that went into a resting one takes all of it, two that met at equal speeds half each. `ParticleStep._sweep_bodies` and `_sweep_delta` mirror it. Why:
   - **The dip lasted, it wasn't a frame.** At 1.6 spacings a substep (main.tscn's Drop buttons, ~390 px/s), the falling carrot's bottom row ended ~2.5 spacings into the lower box. The contacts and push-out move only the members that are in, and shape matching hands each of them a tenth of that (one row of ten), so it took ~12 frames to leave, and the push-out turned the box while it was in: the tilt rose from 0.006 to 0.195 rad over 4 frames as 26–60 members were inside (`boxhigh`, traced frame by frame).
   - **A thin body was gone through.** On a 16 × 2 plank the carrot's bottom row ended between the plank's two rows, where the nearest member is the bottom row, whose outline faces down: the push-out held it there for good (`plank`, CPU and GPU, 26 members inside). The face at the start is the top row's.
   - **The share of the substep, along the body's own path.** Tried first: moving the body back along the face's normal, by the members' mean depth, split by contact mass (`stack_weight`). The mean was half the needed move for a carrot landing flat, whose second row also ends just inside; by contact mass the carrot took 65%, the plank's share went into the floor, and the carrot stayed 0.8 px inside, past `SWEEP_DEPTH`, and went in between the plank's rows in the next substep; and the deepest member over the plank's corner, whose outline's normal is diagonal, moved the carrot back sideways, so it slid off the plank at 74 px/s (main.tscn's geometry, CPU and GPU).
   - **The least depth.** Without it the members over a corner of the box below, which sit on its diagonal outline, went in by ~0.01 px now and then and were rewound, and a 5-box stack leaned 1 px a box and never came to rest (`test_five_aligned_boxes_hold_without_sinking`). Slower approaches are left to the contacts and push-out, which held stacks before.
   - **Not the push's spin** (the handoff's step 2, tried first): adding the push's turn about the centroid to item 13, so the stop-but-don't-launch rule also takes the spin out, left `boxhigh`'s impact tilt at 0.177 rad (0.171 before), since the tilt is the push-out turning the box while it is in, not spin left in its speed. It halved the sideways rebound (CPU 41 → 22 px/s) but took the turn a corner landing should start (`boxtilt` dipped in 6 samples instead of 2), so it was reverted.
15. **Under water a body's whole face is wet, and so are the side walls** (*added 2026-10-13*, after the owner reported: "add 10k sand, drop 10 corks, add 20k water: some of the corks do not rise up"). Item 4 counted a face as wet only where water particles touch it. A cork that landed on the sand bed sinks 6–12 px into it, and under the pool its bottom and lower sides touch grains, not water: the saturated bed holds only 0.02–0.26 of a cell of pore water. So its bottom had no push and the cork stayed on the bed. Now, in `body_pressure_delta`:
   - A boundary member of a body that was wet last substep, not touching another body, takes its **outline face** as wet where the grid holds liquid at its outline point (fill ≥ `BODY_PORE_WET` 0.02) and a pressure over 0, if that face is larger than the one its liquid neighbours give. The face is its way out of the rest shape (item 7), turned by the body's rotation, over half a spacing: 1 on a flat face and √2 along a corner's diagonal, as item 4's wet face reads them. A dry bed reads 0 liquid, so the grid's hydrostatic pressure there pushes nothing. A face where the grid reads 0 is left dry: counted, it took p_ref to 0 in packed corners and the pushes went absolute, as before item 4's bare-member change. Such a face also sets the body's wet flag, so the floor and wall rules stay on where few water particles reach it.
   - **The side walls under a wet body are wet**, as the floor is: a member within 1.5 spacings of a side wall counts the wall as a face of liquid at the grid's pressure there. Without it, a cork on the floor against a wall was pushed into the wall by the water on its other side, and the wall's friction (`solve_apply`) held it.
   - Tried first: counting grains in pore water as wet neighbours (gated by the liquid fill at the member). 5 of 10 corks rose; the stuck ones had 0.45 of their buoyancy, since grains sit off the lattice and leave gaps, and a cork lifted 1 px off the grains lost its bottom's push and settled back (a ratchet; 9 of 10 with the grain reach widened to H, rising after 6–15 s). Leaving the outline face to members with no liquid neighbour at all: 8–10 of 10. Leaving members within 1.5 spacings of a wall to the wall rules: no better. Leaving a face reading 0 out of p_ref when the body is over 32 px deep: no better.
16. **The grid around a body pinned under sand keeps the pool's pressure** (*added 2026-10-13*, after the owner's screenshot: corks in the bottom corners under a sand slope "spazzing up and down" and making "the entire sea jump"). Traced frame by frame in `body_probe sandcorks` (`hydro_solve.glsl`, `ParticleStep._hydro_solve`): the cells around a cork wedged on the floor by sand hold cork, grains and a little water, and three grid rules misread them, each flipping the cell's p every few frames between 0 and 2–90× hydrostatic. The cork's faces read those values (p_ref fell to its floor value 1000 or rose to 170k–5.8M), so it fell and was thrown up at 60–110 px/s, and the solve, which spans the whole tank, carried each flip to all of the water: its mean vertical speed swung ±12 px/s in step with the cork, and up to 2300 water particles moved over 30 px/s. Each rule below was seen in the trace; the three together are what was measured.
   - **No bubble pull in a body's cells** (body volume ≥ `HYDRO_BODY_CELL` 0.05). Water keeps a contact gap from a body, so a cell the cork half fills reads short of full (water 0.31 + body 0.48) with no air in it. Under a full cell it was pulled closed: p 0 on the floor under ~170 px of water. Item 2's pull still closes gaps in cells without body volume.
   - **Sand bed is porous to the grid.** A liquid cell beside sand bed takes the bed's p (the water's continued into the pores) as its neighbour's, instead of the wall term (dp/dn = ρg); at rest that is the same head. Bed cells with body volume ≥ `HYDRO_BODY_CELL` stay a wall, as a body lets no water through. As walls on every side, the sand, the cork, the side wall and the floor closed in a pocket of three cells whose p nothing fixed (any constant solved it): it sat at 11.5k beside 65k of open water, then ran to 5.8 million in one frame when the cork squeezed it (water 0.61 + body 0.40), or to 0 under the pull.
   - **A mixed cell under cover isn't air.** A cell short of bed (sand × 0.7 + body < 0.5) and of `HYDRO_AIR` liquid level, holding at least `HYDRO_MIXED_WET` 0.02 water and `HYDRO_MIXED_SOLID` 0.1 bed fill, under a cell that isn't air, counts as liquid. Beside the cork (water 0.21, sand 0.24, body 0.24) it read as air: a free surface on the floor. Pure water (no bed fill) and dry sand and bodies (no water) are unchanged, so the bubble and surface rules of open water stay as they were.
   - Not run: each rule alone after the first. With the pull rule alone (`sandcorks`, one run), the wedged cork lay still until it began to work loose, and then the pocket's runaway threw it about for ~9 s (the 5.8 million above).
17. **A body's pressure pushes are clamped after their rigid part is taken, not one member at a time** (*added 2026-10-14*, after the owner reported: "add a long horizontal strip of cork, then add 20k water: the cork gets stuck in the bottom of the water, when it should rise to the surface"). `solve_pressure` leaves a body member's push whole (a liquid's is still clamped to half a spacing), and `body_project` replaces every body's pushes by their rigid part (item 12: mean push plus a turn about the centroid), rigid bodies included, clamped to half a spacing per member (`ParticleStep._project_body_pushes`).
   - Why: a 180 × 10 cork (one brush stroke across the screen) on the floor under ~130 px of water had its p_ref, the lowest p over its faces (item 4), swing from one frame to the next between the water's ~45k at its top and 1–19k (`body_probe corkstrip trace refs`): the grid around a wide body that lifts a few px holds voids (air, p = 0) under it and part-filled cells pulled full (item 2's bubble pull, p 3–15k), and a bare or wet member reading one of them set p_ref. Relative to a low p_ref the pushes on its top (~45k) and bottom (~56k) both passed the half-spacing clamp (a cork's clamp is 14,400 of pressure over p_ref), were cut to the same length and cancelled, and the cork sank back. It hopped 1–5 px off the floor at up to 47 px/s for good. Summed before clamping, the absolute pushes cancel over a closed surface and leave the buoyancy, whatever p_ref is. A 16 × 10 cork's p_ref doesn't swing like that, which is why `pourfloor` passed.
   - Rigid bodies too: they were clamped the same way, and the projection gives them what shape matching took from their pushes, without bending them within the substep.
   - Tried first and reverted: (a) the void under a body solved as water in the grid (`hydro_air`): the cork rose, but no water ran in under it; it rose over an air pocket held up by standing walls of water, then fell back. (b) The body reading, in a void or a pulled cell under water, the p of the cell above it continued hydrostatically (`body_p_sample` only, so the water still ran in): p_ref steadied, but the compression the rising cork built in the water above it was copied down to its bottom, the water's resistance pushed it up as well as down, and it shot out of the water at 240 px/s with the whole pool jumping to the ceiling. Also (c) a bare member's outline face wet without pore water (item 15) on top of this item: it left the floor sooner, but this item alone lifts it, so it was left out.

## Considered and rejected
- **Bodies as liquid volume in the grid** (counted in the liquid channel). The grid can't tell a body from water: a dry body on the floor reads as a water column with hydrostatic pressure, and a floating body as a mound of water up to its top, whose extra pressure under it grows as it rises (worked through on paper, not run).
- **The grid's push on every body particle (−∇p dt² / ρ_b)**, with body cells as bed. Bed cells carry a hydrostatic pressure even when dry (it bootstraps from the floor up), so a dry body on the floor or on dry sand would be lifted. Fixing that in the bed cells changes the tuned sand-and-water scenes, and `hydro_solve` precomputes each cell's update from φ alone, so a rule that depends on p can't go into it.
- **Buoyancy from contacts and mass ratios alone** (FleX 7.1). It relies on depth pressure being carried by PBF compression; here the grid carries it, and water at rest is at rest density, so there is nothing to push the body up.
- **A per-body buoyancy force in the body pass** (count submerged members, push all of them up). Needs a "submerged" test per member, which the grid can't give for small bodies, and loses the righting torque.
- **For the push-out: the direction from the grain to the body centroid, or from the members around it.** The centroid points out the wrong side for a grain near a long box's end; the members around a grain inside a lattice square are what cancel today.
- **The push-out shared with the body (mass-weighted).** The grain would need the count of members it sits among to split the push, which it doesn't know; the ordinary contacts already carry the impact to the body.

- **The sweep rewinds a body's whole move.** A body that touches another while sliding fast along it loses that substep's share of its slide too. Not seen in the scenarios (the impacts are head-on); it acts only on members going in at least `SWEEP_DEPTH` in a substep (60 px/s at 2 substeps).
